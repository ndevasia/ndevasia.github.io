Hi there, I'm Nisha! I research how video games can change lives for the better. I received my B.S. from MIT and wrote software in the gaming industry for two years. Currently, I'm doing my PhD in human-computer interaction at the University of Washington and a graduate researcher at foundry10.

Research

I am a player-computer interaction researcher who focuses on supporting eudaimonic gaming experiences in everyday play. Eudaimonic gaming experiences facilitate personal growth, perspective changes, and connection with others, especially for adolescent players. I am particularly interested in how we can scaffold everyday play through custom overlays and tools that support higher-order reflection, and primarily conduct my research through building and deploying such tools.

I am a PhD candidate at the University of Washington, co-advised by Dr. Julie Kientz (Human Centered Design & Engineering) and Dr. Jin Ha Lee (Information School). I was formerly an undergraduate researcher at the MIT Media Lab (supervised by Dr. Safinah Ali and advised by Dr. Cynthia Breazeal), where I researched social robots as creativity support tools for children and helped develop foundational curricula for AI literacy.

I am also a graduate researcher at foundry10, where I conduct research and create programs for play-based learning initiatives with local youth and community partners.

    Backend engineering on Twitch's video networking team

    I spent two years wrestling with the complexities of the Twitch video system and doing my best to contribute useful services where I can. Below are some brief summaries of the projects I worked on. Several details are omitted due to non-disclosure agreement.

    Porting distributed locking system to AWS DynamoDB

    • Project: Amazon IVS
    • Timeline: March 2022 - August 2023

    In order to increase the redundancy of a service (details omitted), I had to port its distributed locking system from a third party service to AWS DynamoDB so that multiple entities could view the lock state of other entities around them. This required a deep understanding of how the system's locking actually worked, and writing rigorous tests to ensure that the overhaul was actually correct. To use a good phrase from a mentor of mine, I felt that this project bizarrely validated most traditional computer science undergraduate curricula.

    Minimal-effort packet disappearance detection

    • Project: Amazon IVS
    • Timeline: August 2021 - August 2022

    My first project as a junior engineer was to build a service that detects abrupt drops in certain arenas of the video system's traffic. These drops are colloquially referred to as 'black holes', and the service is somewhat cheekily called LIGO. LIGO runs on AWS ECS/Fargate and connects to SQS, Kinesis, and Apache Druid. I architected, coded, and productionized the system entirely on my own, and gained a lot of experience on the journey.

    Implementing unequal cost multipathing

    • Project: Amazon IVS
    • Timeline: January 2022 - April 2022
    • Mentors: Rob Mitchelmore

    Unequal cost multipathing, or UCMP, allows more efficient allocation of network traffic across the video system. I updated the previous algorithm (which implemented equal cost multipathing, UCMP's more inefficent predecessor) and worked with a colleague on the networking team to test the code live on the network. This project was yet another step in my journey to understand how exactly the internet works, something that I likely will never understand.

    Data pipelining service for real-time estimated viewership on third party CDNs

    • Project: Amazon IVS
    • Timeline: June 2020 - August 2020
    • Mentors: Kyle Lee, Jos Kraaijeveld

    As an intern in the summer of 2020, I was tasked with building a service to track the Twitch video system's usage of third party CDNs which output a graph showing how many users were routed to CDNs per minute. I got my first taste of assembling AWS services into a single working program, and utilized Kinesis, Lambda, and CloudWatch.

    Designing and writing curriculum for a GANs textbook chapter

    Kids growing up in the age of AI need accessible online platforms with which to learn advanced concepts that will broaden their understanding of the computerized ecosystem they live in. The goal of GenAI was to design such a platform for learners of all levels. We intended to create a prototype to hand off to professional designers.

    • Project: GenAI Initiative
    • Timeline: October 2019 - June 2020
    • Team: Katherine Gallagher

    The platform GenAI was intended to be an online textbook with several chapters about different AI topics, such as neural networks, conversational agents, and robotics, among several others. The chapter we decided to prototype with was the one about generative adversarial networks (GANs) due to the project lead's personal interest in them.

    A great deal of time was spent on the interaction design of the website itself, and keeping both the landing pages and the learning material itself as easy to navigate as possible for learners and users of all ages and levels. For example, we determined that while the color scheme and design should remain the same throughout the website, the "feel" (font, UI) should change between content levels.

    We also wanted to make AI seem approachable, not futuristic, dark, chrome colored, or hacker-y. We considered naturalistic themes and light colors in opposition to the typical depiction of AI in media.

    At the beginning of the project, I worked along with another student in charge of web design to wireframe out the look and feel of the website. I went through several of the educational websites that I used on a daily basis and tried to pick out the elements that I found the most natural and usable, and then combine these into a design. The web designer took my fairly simple ideas and gave them a great deal of color.





    Halfway through the project, however, the web designer got too busy, and I was left with coming up with the visual design elements of the website as well. In accordance with our naturalistic ideas for representing AI, I chose a color palette, designed a v1 logo, and created a moodboard.

    I was also assigned to write the beginner and intermediate levels of a GANs textbook chapter. This was the first time that I had to think deeply about what it meant to be a learner at each of these levels, and be mindful to use appropriate terminology and complexity. Similarly, we designed the visuals to "level up" as well.



    This project taught me a great deal about designing UI and thinking very deeply about my user. Though I had never dabbled in visual design before, I enjoyed the chance to determine how the site looked and felt, skills that proved extremely useful to designing this website!

    Redesigning Santa Monica Studio's engine debugging system

    When creating a game as huge as God of War, designers do an almost unimaginable amount of tweaking and twiddling with different game engine properties to get the gameplay feeling just right. My task as an intern was to create a prototype for an updated engine debugging menu. Several details are omitted due to non-disclosure agreement.

    • Project: God of War: Ragnarok
    • Timeline: May 2019 - August 2019
    • Mentor: Alden Chew

    Like many triple A game studios, Santa Monica Studio utilizes a custom built engine for God of War. As such, the tool for tweaking settings within said engine was also custom designed, and was over a decade old when I was tasked with creating a more modern prototype.

    I was tasked with using the Dear ImGui library to create this prototype. ImGui is an open source C++ library for creating user interfaces, primarily for game engines and other real-time 3D applications.



    This was my first time working with a codebase on a massive scale. I learned how to parse through huge chunks of relatively undocumented code, and gained an intuition for how to look for the code actually relevant to my needs. I had also never worked with an external library as big as ImGui, so figuring out how to integrate it into the existing PlayStation codebase was a slow task.

    I also learned what it was like to have multiple stakeholders for a system. Designers, artists, and programmers all used the engine debugging tool in different ways, and had different opinions on the changes that should be made to it. From the way the PS4 controller was used to navigate the menu, to the actual widgets used for certain features themselves, I learned the importance of taking multiple voices into account when creating an end product for a variety of users.

    The biggest challenge I faced was struggling through the nuances of C++. I learned that low-level programming is definitely not my forte, and the intricacies of buffer overflows and overwritten caches did not bring me much joy. But in the end, the experience of working in a triple A studio on such a huge project was worth all the frustration. As a small contributor, I'm excited for the world to see God of War: Ragnarok, and as a fan of the franchise, I'm dying to play it myself!

    EDC: Developing data science curriculum for high school sciences

    I was a design collaborator for the Science+C initiative through the Educational Development Center. I designed a three day data science curriculum for integration into high school biology, chemistry, and physics classes. I ran workshops with educators and iterated using their feedback. The curriculum will be used by the State of Massachusetts.

    • Project: Science+C
    • Timeline: December 2020 - February 2021
    • Team: Irene Lee (PI), Claire Bao, Grace Kim, Erica Zhou

    The Science+C project is a collaboration between the Massachusetts Department of Elementary and Secondary Education to create an alternate pathway to college and science careers for high school students by developing and testing three new computational science courses. These courses—Computational Biology, Computational Chemistry, and Computational Physics—will be available to all Massachusetts high schools beginning in the 2022-2023 school year.


    I specifically worked as a design collaborator on the Data Science + Machine Learning units. The units were divided into data science, decision trees, clustering algorithms, and neural networks. I designed the data science curriculum, a 3 day workshop with a lesson and associated activities for each day.



    I created lesson plans designed to introduce beginners to data science at a low level. I cover the basics of data science and what data scientists to, and designed activities to teach summary statistics and visualization usage in an interactive manner.



    Activities included:
    • An unplugged activity in which students provide their own data and organize themselves into a dataset to learn summary statistics.
    • An adapted memory game for matching certain situations to the data visualization that would be the most appropriate.
    • Multiple R programming activities written in Google CoLab that walk students through analyzing datasets relevant to their high school science class.

    I ran this curriculum in a workshop with several high school science educators and iterated on the activities with them. Their feedback was invaluable for iterating and improving on the activities.






    The curriculum will continue to go through iteration cycles until it pilots with schools across the state of Massachusetts in September 2022.

    Open Collectives: Architecture for an Equitable Digital Economy

    As part of the Civic Media Codesign Studio, I learned theory and practice of codesign, including methods for community participation in iterative stages of project ideation, design, prototyping, testing, launch, and stewardship. I was part of the research team that investigated collectives throughout history, and conducted sensory ethnographic interviews to construct transmedia narratives for community partners. Our work was part of a display at the Venice Biennale 2021.

    • Project: Open Collectives
    • Timeline: February 2020 - May 2020
    • Team: Sarah Rege, Adiel Benitez, Darla Earl

    Open Collectives is ana initiative that seeks to "leverage the power of solidarity in order to strengthen economic sovereignty, housing affordability, communal self-determination, and mutual aid". As part of the class working on the initiative, I worked on the CarePod project (now Carehaus) team to develop stories and visual media for the initiative. The goal of CarePod is to developing intergenerational co-housing where caregivers live alongside and care for older adults and disabled people.


    We started our project by conducting an in-depth sensory ethnographic interview with an individual who gave up his career to take care of his grandmother, both out of familial obligation and a lack of other options. From this interview, we were told to construct a story told from the point of view of an object important to the interviewee's experience with their aging relative. We chose to focus on a rocking chair that was a prized possession of the interviewee's grandmother, but unfortunately, as she aged, she no longer was able to stand up if she sat down in it.

    In addition to the media we created, we conducted research on the aging population in the United States and how we could rethink elder care in times of crisis. The class was being run right at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, so finding affordable housing models for both the elderly and their care workers was a particularly salient issue.


    Over the course of the semester, our team produced five stories, to which I contributed two. Our research and the visual/written media that we produced were part of a display at the Venice Biennale in 2021.

    Teaching misinformation literacy worldwide

    Part of my research appointment as a PhD student is with the Universty of Washington's Center for an Informed Public, one of the premier centers for misinformation research. As part of the Loki's Loop team, I help create and co-design media literacy games with stakeholders all over the world. As belief in misinformation is a cultural and socioemotional phenomenon, we believe that co-design helps us tailor educational experiences to specific contexts. Below are a few of the sub-projects I've worked on for the team.

    Developing portable co-design curriculum and workshop

    • Project: Loki's Loop
    • Timeline: January 2025 - present
    • Mentors: Chris Coward, Jin Ha Lee

    While our team has used co-design methods in the past to 'reskin' our games for different cultural contexts (published in the DiGRA 2025 proceedings), we desired a method to efficiently design game scenarios from the ground up with input from our stakeholders. I worked with a capstone team from the MLIS program to create a curriculum based off of my design framework (described below). We currently plan to test this curriculum in a 2-day workshop format in the Netherlands and at the NextLibrary festival in Denmark.

    Mixed-methods comparison of narrative engagement in our games

    • Project: Loki's Loop
    • Timeline: March 2024 - September 2024
    • Mentors: Jin Ha Lee

    Building on my hypothesis that the narrative mechanic in misinformation education games must be of some consequence, I conducted a 2x2 between-subjects study using two of the Loki's Loop games, which are identical except for their narrative framings. The participants were split by how much they identified with the subject matter of the second game (online fandom and Twitter usage). The study was primarily quantitative, measuring narrative engagement as the dependent variable. There were indeed differences of consequence, which I discuss in my paper accepted to CHI 2025, the premier conference in human-computer interaction.

    Narrative design framework for misinformation education games

    • Project: Loki's Loop
    • Timeline: November 2023 - September 2024
    • Mentors: Jin Ha Lee

    The narrative mechanics of educational games are often overlooked. In misinformation education, I believed this to be a particularly large miss, as the stories we tell ourselves become what we believe (and in many cases, what we believe is misinformation). This led me to believe that most misinformation education games are focusing on the wrong thing: using rational processes to discern what is and isn't misinformation, rather than exploring how sociocultural narratives may affect our own beliefs. I did a deep dive into narrative theory, and performed a systematic review on the current body of misinformation education games to see how they use narratives to teach about misinformation. The discussion, as well as the resulting design framework, was published in the Harvard Misinformation Review.

    Designing for playing games together in long-distance relationships

    When I started grad school, I moved 1000 miles and 2 states away from my partner doing his PhD at Stanford. To cope with the sudden change in distance, we started playing a lot of Stardew Valley together. As a researcher, I wanted to know what my field had to say about technology and long distance relationships, as this was now my only way of staying in contact. Surprisingly, there wasn't much that was applicable outside of a lab format, or custom artifacts that had been built in research settings. There wasn't much on the use of existing technologies that people use to stay connected, most notably games. Almost everybody I know in an LDR plays games with their partner as quality time together. This was the beginning of what I consider to be my most passion-driven research project.

    Qualitative investigation of how couples in LDRs play games together

    • Timeline: January 2024 - June 2024
    • Mentors: Julie Kientz

    In my first ever self-initiated project as a PhD student, I conducted a diary study and semi-structured interviews with 13 couples from all over the world in LDRs. Through a combination of inductive and deductive analysis, we extracted key themes surrounding the affordances of cooperative vs. competitive multiplayer games, the liminality between in-person and in-game social interactions, and the appropriation of game mechanics to express affection.

    I also conducted a design activity based on the MDA framework where I had couples assemble game elements to envision their ideal game to play together. This led to some wholesome brainstorming sessions, shown below. This work is published in the DIS conference proceedings and you can read the full paper here.

    How games support mental health and eudaimonic well-being

    I'm only in my mid 20s, but people my age have lived through a huge amount of unrest - the first Trump administration, COVID, and the second - much more fascist - Trump administration, with very little mental break in between. In both personal and environmental times of darkness, I find myself going back to the games that have taught me strength, given me characters to model myself after, and given me a place where I feel like I can fight back against evil. I firmly believe that playing games like Final Fantasy VII in my formative years - all about how a giant corporation destroys the world through capitalism and greed - has been key to shaping my own will to live on and fight on. With that in mind, the area I hope to write my dissertation on someday is in how playing games can support eudaimonic well-being, human flourishing, and mental health. Games for mental health are often discussed in the language of escapism, but I think there are a lot of important lessons we can learn from our ability to agentically interact with stories of resistance, resilience, and heroism within games.

    Investigating the effects of eudaimonic gaming experiences

    • Timeline: October 2024 - present
    • Mentors: Julie Kientz, Jin Ha Lee

    After wading around in readings about self-determination theory, narrative games, and eudaimonic well-being, I identified a theoretical gap that had mysteriously gone unfilled. Media studies scholars have elaborated extensively on why a person may seek out a eudaimonic gaming experience, but the long lasting effects of such experiences were unknown. Partly motivated by getting to read about peoples' positive and life-changing experiences with games, I conducted a large survey that received 100+ responses, and conducted a structured deductive analysis to identify pathways to certain types of changes that people believed these gaming experiences had on their broader lives. Our findings support conjectures floating around in the HCI game space, namely that 1. often, meaningful experiences are incidental rather than intentional 2. people undergoing times of stress may actively be seeking meaning in their gaming experiences and 3. narrative elements in games are influential on people's perceptions of themselves and the world. The full paper is published in CHI PLAY 2025.

    Systematic review on narrative games for mental health

    • Timeline: June 2024 - present
    • Mentors: Julie Kientz

    The games that have supported my mental health have always been narrative heavy RPGs. In my first year, I took a particular interest in narrative games, and like a good junior PhD student, I decided to do my first systematic review on how narrative game-based interveions are used in mental health research. Myself and Jamie Espinosa-Briones (DUB REU '24) reduced almost 2000 papers down to 17 games for analysis. A preliminary analysis (presented at DiGRA '25) revealed that a majority of the games surrounded treatment for depression and anxiety; however, how these constructs were measured differed significantly between studies. In addition, theoretical underpinnings such as narrative theory (popuarlized by communications scholars) were often overlooked in the design of these games. In future discussion (journal paper coming soon), we intend to suggest design guidelines for further cross-collaboration between communication studies, mental health, and human-computer interaction.

    Facilitating the study of reflection in games

    My previous work (as well as existing research) has shown that games can affect people's wellbeing positively, but these changes often occur over the course of months and years. While we can have players recount in retrospect how the games they have played have affected them, it is difficult to make any conclusions about this without knowing what they were thinking while playing.

    An additional issue is that people generally aren't very critical or reflective of the media they interface with. While we are taught, very thoroughly, how to critically reflect on books over the course of our K-12 education, we aren't taught to do the same with games. So: how can we make steps towards teaching people to be more reflective on the games they play, and how might that affect their perceptions of their own play and well-being as related to play?

    Developing SAVEstate and SavePoint

    • Timeline: August 2025 - present
    • Mentors: Julie Kientz, Jin Ha Lee, Safinah Ali

    Heavily inspired by The Well-Played Game by Tracy Fullerton and Matthew Farber, I determined that it would be helpful to be able to annotate my own gameplay, just as I had been taught to annotate the books I read. This led to the development of a tool called SavePoint, which you can see demos of below. SavePoint is currently under development to be used as part of a player experience method that I am developing for my research, which is currently under submission so I can't talk about it too much. However, beyond its utility as a research tool, SavePoint is also useful for people who (like myself) just want to keep notes on the games that they're playing for later review.
